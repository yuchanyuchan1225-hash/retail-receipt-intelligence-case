01 / The business problem

Revenue was visible.
Customer purchase behaviour was not.

Existing transaction data could show totals, but not reliably answer who bought which brands, categories, price bands, or combinations. That made member activation, brand collaboration, and repeat-purchase initiatives dependent on assumptions.

Before

Store purchases were isolated transactions. Sales reporting existed, yet there was limited reusable purchase intelligence at a customer level.

The target state

Link a member identity to receipt evidence and turn brand, category, amount, and time into structured signals for the next operating decision.

02 / Product decision

Why receipt-led capture,
instead of waiting for POS integration?

The decision was not to choose the most technically ambitious solution. It was to choose the quickest credible route to validate real purchase data and an operating model under real organisational constraints.

Choice made

Use customer-submitted receipts to prove value first.

Direct POS or loyalty-platform integration remains a valuable long-term route, but crosses systems, departments, and vendors. Receipt upload gave the business a lower-friction way to test data quality, incentives, and operational feasibility before making a larger integration bet.

Options not prioritised

Direct POS integration carried a longer coordination cycle. Staff entry introduced frontline burden and inconsistent data quality. Registration or surveys alone could not confirm actual purchases.

The operating choice

Membership ID, receipt image, OCR, review, points, and gifts form one loop. The design creates evidence now while keeping room for future POS, e-commerce, and loyalty connections.

03 / Operating model

Built as an operating loop,
not just a customer feature.

Customer upload is only the entry point. Review, exception handling, reward allocation, and analytical reuse determine whether the product can function in daily operations.

The model retained manual paths for duplicates, amount anomalies, and uncertain OCR outputs. The goal was controlled accuracy and operability, not automation theatre.

04 / Technical governance

I did not write the code.
I did shape the technical decision.

An external delivery partner implemented the software. My role was to translate business and operating requirements into acceptance criteria, boundaries, trade-offs, and delivery decisions across scope, quality, cost, and sequence.

Customer layer: registration / receipt upload / reward visibility
Operations layer: review / member lookup / campaigns / redemption
Business services: OCR / points / gifts / data outputs
Run layer: API / database / cache / image storage / containerised services

Governance decisions I contributed to

  • Separate customer and operations experiences so day-to-day work remains executable
  • Evaluate OCR on usable business fields and exceptions, not on recognition alone
  • Require reconciliation and human review before OCR outputs can trigger rewards
  • Control first-phase scope while preserving boundaries for POS and e-commerce integration

Quality model

Acceptance focused on totals, tax, item detail, timing, reconciliation, and traceable exception handling.

Vendor management

Decisions considered operating load, extensibility, cost, delivery sequence, and ownership—not only a feature checklist.

Data design

Requirements covered image evidence, OCR state, purchase time, amount, item data, points, review state, and anomaly flags.

06 / What changed

Move from sales reporting
to customer intelligence.

What was validated

The project established a practical link between customer identity and real purchase evidence. Brand mix, category, price band, basket composition, and customer value can now become inputs to CRM decisions rather than remaining isolated transactions.

What comes next

Member segmentation, annual accumulation, targeted rewards, e-commerce activation, and deeper data connections are next-phase opportunities. They are presented as plans, not completed outcomes.
